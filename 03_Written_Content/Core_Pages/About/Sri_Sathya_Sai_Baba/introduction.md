# Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba

## An Introduction

How should a person encounter the life of Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba for the first time?

Through the millions who came to see Him?

Through the schools, universities, hospitals and drinking-water projects established under His guidance?

Through the international spiritual movement that grew around His teachings?

Through the accounts of those who experienced His compassion personally?

Or through the simple teachings for which He became known around the world:

**Love All. Serve All.**

**Help Ever. Hurt Never.**

Each offers a way to begin.

None, by itself, tells the whole story.

Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba was born Sathya Narayana Raju on 23 November 1926 in Puttaparthi, a village in southern India. From His youth, He declared a spiritual mission centred upon the transformation of the individual, the unity of religions, selfless service and the recognition of the divine nature inherent within every human being.

Over the decades that followed, Puttaparthi became the centre of a worldwide spiritual and humanitarian movement.

People came from different nations, cultures, professions and faiths.

Some came with devotion.

Some with questions.

Some were drawn by His teachings.

Others by His humanitarian work.

Many encountered a message that was at once profoundly spiritual and remarkably practical:

Human life has a higher purpose.

Character matters.

Love must be expressed through action.

Service to others can become a path of self-transformation.

All religions deserve respect.

And the divinity we seek is not distant from us, but inherent within us.

## Why “Bhagawan”?

Devotees commonly address Sri Sathya Sai Baba as **Bhagawan**, a Sanskrit honorific referring to the Divine.

For those encountering His life for the first time, it is helpful to understand that people approach Sri Sathya Sai Baba from different perspectives.

Millions revere Him as an Avatar — a divine incarnation.

Others encounter Him first as a spiritual teacher, humanitarian or educator.

The purpose of these pages is not to demand a particular starting point.

It is to invite sincere exploration.

To understand His life.

To engage with His teachings.

To examine His work.

And, most importantly, to consider the questions He placed before humanity.

What is the purpose of human life?

What makes education meaningful?

What is the relationship between spirituality and service?

Can religions coexist without competition?

What happens when love moves beyond emotion and becomes action?

And who are we, beneath the identities we accumulate?

## A Spiritual Teacher for Everyday Life

Bhagawan's teachings ranged across an extraordinary breadth of human experience.

The mind.

Character.

Education.

Religion.

Family life.

Work.

Leadership.

Health.

Society.

Devotion.

Service.

Self-knowledge.

Yet a consistent principle ran through them all:

**Spirituality must be lived.**

It is not enough to speak about love.

Love must influence how we treat people.

It is not enough to admire truth.

Truth must enter our words and actions.

It is not enough to discuss peace.

We must examine the desires, attachments and reactions that disturb peace within us.

It is not enough to worship the Divine in a particular form.

We must learn to recognise the same divine presence in others.

This emphasis on practice gave His teachings a distinctive immediacy.

The spiritual journey did not require abandoning ordinary life.

Ordinary life became the place where spiritual growth occurred.

At home.

At school.

At work.

In relationships.

During difficulties.

While serving others.

And in the quiet examination of our own thoughts and motives.

## The Five Human Values

Central to Bhagawan's educational and spiritual message are five universal Human Values:

**Truth — Sathya**

**Right Conduct — Dharma**

**Peace — Shanti**

**Love — Prema**

**Non-violence — Ahimsa**

These values were not presented merely as ideals to admire.

Bhagawan taught that they are inherent within the human being.

The task of education and spiritual practice is to help bring them into expression.

Truth gives direction to thought and speech.

Right Conduct translates values into action.

Peace emerges through inner discipline.

Love expands beyond self-interest.

Non-violence becomes the fullest expression of love in thought, word and deed.

Together, the Human Values provide a practical framework for individual character and social responsibility.

## The Unity of Faiths

Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba did not establish a new religion.

He taught respect for all faiths and encouraged people to practise their own religion sincerely.

The unity of religions was not, in His teaching, the claim that every tradition is identical.

It was the recognition that beneath different names, forms, practices and cultures lies a shared spiritual aspiration towards truth, love, righteousness and communion with the Divine.

This principle remains fundamental to the Sathya Sai movement.

No one should be pressured to abandon one faith for another.

No religion should be diminished to elevate another.

Spiritual growth should deepen respect.

Not division.

## Love Made Visible

Perhaps the clearest way to understand Bhagawan's mission is to consider the relationship between His teachings and His work.

He spoke about compassion.

Then institutions providing healthcare without charge were established.

He spoke about the purpose of education.

Then schools and a university integrating academic excellence with character development were established.

He spoke about serving those in need.

Then major drinking-water projects brought safe water to communities facing severe scarcity.

He spoke about the unity of humanity.

Then people from nations and religions across the world came together in service.

The institutions matter.

The scale matters.

The outcomes matter.

But the principle connecting them matters even more:

**Love must become useful.**

It must feed.

Heal.

Educate.

Protect dignity.

Relieve suffering.

And inspire one human being to care for another.

## His Relationship With Young People

Young people occupied an important place in Bhagawan's mission.

He spoke frequently about character, courage, discipline, education, leadership and the responsibility of youth.

He did not ask young people merely to prepare for some distant future.

He challenged them to become examples now.

To use education for the benefit of society.

To develop self-confidence grounded in knowledge of the Self.

To control desires rather than become controlled by them.

To respect parents and elders.

To serve those in need.

To develop unity between thought, word and action.

And to recognise that the strength of a nation depends ultimately upon the character of its people.

For MySAIYa, these teachings remain deeply relevant.

Young adulthood is filled with questions about identity, relationships, work, achievement, uncertainty and purpose.

Bhagawan's message does not remove these questions.

It invites us to approach them differently.

With values.

With self-examination.

With courage.

With compassion.

And with a deeper understanding of who we are.

---

## Beyond Admiration

It is possible to admire an extraordinary life from a distance.

Bhagawan continually directed attention closer to home.

Towards our own lives.

Our habits.

Our motives.

Our relationships.

Our choices.

Our character.

The enduring invitation of His life is therefore not simply:

**Look at what was accomplished.**

It is:

**What will you do with the life you have been given?**

Can you love more widely?

Serve more selflessly?

Speak more truthfully?

Live more consciously?

Respect differences more deeply?

Use your abilities for the good of others?

And move, however gradually, towards understanding your true nature?

That is where exploration becomes personal.

That is where His life meets ours.
